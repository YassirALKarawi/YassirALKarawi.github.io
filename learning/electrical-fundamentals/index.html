STEP 1 / 5

1. Identify the series path

تحديد التوالي

All three resistors lie on one unbranched path. The same current passes through each resistor. No current is lost as it passes through a resistor.

Given: Vs = 12 V; R₁ = 100 Ω; R₂ = 200 Ω; R₃ = 300 Ω.
STEP 2 / 5

2. Replace the chain by one resistor

رسم الدائرة المكافئة

Add the three resistances. Replace the chain by R_eq while preserving the two external terminals. The source sees the same voltage–current relationship.

Req = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ = 100 + 200 + 300 = 600 Ω.
STEP 3 / 5

3. Calculate the total current

حساب التيار الكلي

Apply Ohm’s law to the equivalent circuit. This is also the current through every resistor in the original circuit.

Itotal = Vs / Req = 12 / 600 = 0.020 A = 20 mA.
STEP 4 / 5

4. Restore the resistors and find their voltages

العودة إلى الدائرة الأصلية وحساب الجهود

Now return to the original resistors. Multiply the common current by each resistance. To find the voltage on R₂, use its own resistance, not the total resistance.

V₁ = IR₁ = 0.020 × 100 = 2 V.
V₂ = IR₂ = 0.020 × 200 = 4 V.
V₃ = IR₃ = 0.020 × 300 = 6 V.
STEP 5 / 5

5. Check Kirchhoff’s voltage law

التحقق من مجموع الجهود

Travel around the complete loop. The voltage rise from the source equals the sum of resistor drops. The resistor with the largest resistance has the largest voltage drop because all resistors share current.

12 − 2 − 4 − 6 = 0 V.
Voltage-divider check: V₂ = 12 × 200/600 = 4 V.

STEP 1 / 5

1. Identify the common pair of nodes

تحديد التوازي

Each resistor connects to the same upper and lower nodes. Therefore each branch has the full 12 V source voltage. The animation shows current splitting at the upper node and recombining at the lower node.

V₁ = V₂ = V₃ = Vs = 12 V.
STEP 2 / 5

2. Combine the conductances

حساب المقاومة المكافئة

Add reciprocals and then invert the sum. Adding the resistance values themselves would give a series result and would be wrong here. The equivalent must be below 200 Ω, the smallest branch resistance.

1/Req = 1/200 + 1/300 + 1/600
= 3/600 + 2/600 + 1/600 = 6/600 = 1/100.
Req = 100 Ω.
STEP 3 / 5

3. Calculate source current

حساب تيار المصدر

Ohm’s law on the equivalent circuit gives the current supplied by the source. This current is the sum of the three branch currents, not the current through each resistor.

Itotal = Vs/Req = 12/100 = 0.120 A = 120 mA.
STEP 4 / 5

4. Restore each branch and apply Ohm’s law

حساب تيار كل فرع

Every branch sees 12 V. Divide this common voltage by the individual branch resistance. A smaller resistance carries a larger current. In this example the branch currents are in the ratio 3:2:1.

I₁ = 12/200 = 0.060 A = 60 mA.
I₂ = 12/300 = 0.040 A = 40 mA.
I₃ = 12/600 = 0.020 A = 20 mA.
STEP 5 / 5

5. Check current conservation

التحقق من مجموع التيارات

At the source node, outgoing branch currents must sum to the incoming source current. You can also verify any branch using its conductance fraction. Relative branch motion speeds follow the 3:2:1 current ratio.

Itotal = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ = 60 + 40 + 20 = 120 mA.
I₂ = Itotal × (1/R₂)/(1/R₁+1/R₂+1/R₃)
= 120 × (1/300)/(1/100) = 40 mA.

The intermediate circuit now has two series resistors. Add them, draw the final equivalent and use the source voltage to find total current.

STEP 4 / 5

4. Expand one level to recover group voltage

حساب جهد المجموعة المتوازية

V₂₃ = ItotalR₂₃ = 0.040 × 200 = 8 V.

The voltage on R₂ is 8 V, not 12 V. R₁ already accounts for a 4 V drop. Apply Ohm’s law separately to the restored branches and verify that their currents sum to 40 mA.

V₂ = V₃ = V₂₃ = 8 V.
I₂ = 8/300 = 26.666… mA.
I₃ = 8/600 = 13.333… mA.
I₂ + I₃ = 40 mA = Itotal.

Lecture 01 · Electrical Engineering I

01. Charge, Current and SI Units

الشحنة والتيار

1. Physical understanding

Charge q is measured in coulombs. Current is the rate at which charge crosses a chosen surface, rather than the amount of charge stored in a component. A reference arrow defines positive current. Conventional current points in the direction positive charge would move, opposite to electron drift in a metal.

2. Mathematical model

i = dq/dt; q(t) = q(0) + ∫ i(τ) dτ

i: current (A), q: charge (C), t: time (s). The integral is a signed accumulated charge.

3. Worked example

A conductor carries 250 mA for 8 s. How much charge crosses it?

  1. Convert 250 mA to 0.250 A.
  2. For constant current, q = It.
  3. q = 0.250 × 8 = 2 C.
2 C

4. Choose a reference before calculating

Begin by drawing one current arrow. If q(t) = 3t² mC, differentiate with respect to time to obtain i(t) = 6t mA. At t = 2 s the instantaneous current is 12 mA, whereas the average over 0–2 s is [q(2)−q(0)]/2 = 6 mA. The distinction matters whenever charge transfer is not uniform.

Analysis method. Distinguish instantaneous current from average current. Use charge differences for an interval and a derivative at an instant.

Second worked example

A steady 4 mA passes for 30 s.

q = 0.004 × 30 = 0.12 C.

Common mistake. Charge is not consumed by a resistor. Energy is transferred while charge is conserved.

5. Check your understanding

If 6 C crosses in 3 s, find the average current.

2 A. Divide transferred charge by elapsed time.

Does a negative current mean the calculation is wrong?

No. It means the actual conventional current opposes the selected reference arrow.

Lecture 02 · Electrical Engineering I

02. Voltage, Power and Energy

الجهد والقدرة والطاقة

1. Physical understanding

Voltage is energy transferred per unit charge between two terminals. Choose a polarity before calculating. Under the passive sign convention, current enters the positive voltage terminal. Positive p = vi then means the element absorbs energy. A source delivering energy has negative absorbed power. The sum of the signed powers in a closed circuit is zero.

2. Mathematical model

v = dw/dq; p = vi; W = ∫ p(t) dt

v: voltage (V), w and W: energy (J), p: power (W). Use the passive sign convention.

3. Worked example

A 12 V load draws 0.5 A for 2 min. Find its power and energy.

  1. Power = 12 × 0.5 = 6 W.
  2. Convert 2 min to 120 s.
  3. Energy = 6 × 120 = 720 J.
6 W; 720 J

4. Audit the energy balance

For a 12 V source supplying a 24 Ω resistor, I = 0.5 A. The resistor absorbs +6 W because current enters its positive terminal. At the source, current leaves the positive terminal, giving absorbed power −6 W. Their algebraic sum is zero. If your total signed power is nonzero, revisit a polarity, arrow or arithmetic step.

Analysis method. Choose a voltage polarity and a current reference for every element. Never remove a negative sign just because power sounds positive.

Second worked example

A device absorbs 3 W for 5 min.

W = 3 × 300 = 900 J.

Common mistake. Energy and power are different quantities. A watt is a joule per second.

5. Check your understanding

A 5 V element carries 0.2 A entering its negative terminal. Find absorbed power.

−1 W. The element delivers 1 W.

Which quantity describes energy transferred per second?

Power, measured in watts.

Lecture 03 · Electrical Engineering I

03. Resistance, Conductance and Ohm’s Law

المقاومة وقانون أوم

1. Physical understanding

An ideal linear resistor relates terminal voltage to current. Its resistance remains constant in this model. Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance and is measured in siemens. A real resistor also has tolerance and a power rating. Excess power causes heating, so calculation of current must be followed by a rating check. For a uniform conductor, longer length raises resistance and larger area reduces it.

2. Mathematical model

v = iR; G = 1/R; R = ρℓ/A; P = i²R = v²/R

R: resistance (Ω), G: conductance (S), ρ: resistivity (Ω m), ℓ: length (m), A: area (m²).

3. Worked example

Apply 9 V to a 300 Ω resistor. Select a suitable rating from 0.125 W, 0.25 W and 0.5 W.

  1. I = 9/300 = 0.030 A.
  2. P = 81/300 = 0.27 W.
  3. Only 0.5 W exceeds the computed dissipation among the available ratings.
30 mA; 0.27 W; choose 0.5 W

4. Read a component specification

A nominal 1 kΩ resistor with ±5% tolerance may have a resistance between 950 Ω and 1050 Ω. At 10 V, the largest current occurs at the smallest resistance: 10/950 = 10.526 mA. Its largest dissipation is 100/950 = 0.10526 W. Tolerance affects current and thermal checks even when the ideal model is linear.

Analysis method. State whether the resistor is ideal or specified with tolerance. Convert kΩ to Ω before combining with volts and amperes.

Second worked example

Find the conductance of 250 Ω.

G = 1/250 = 0.004 S = 4 mS.

Common mistake. Do not apply a constant resistance model to every nonlinear component, such as a diode.

5. Check your understanding

A 2 kΩ resistor has 4 V across it. Find current and power.

2 mA and 8 mW.

At fixed voltage, what happens when resistance doubles?

Current halves and resistor power halves.

Lecture 04 · Electrical Engineering I

04. Nodes, Branches and Kirchhoff’s Laws

قوانين كيرشوف

1. Physical understanding

A node is a set of points connected by ideal wire and therefore at one potential. A branch is a connection between nodes containing an element or a series group. A loop is a closed path. Kirchhoff’s current law (KCL) expresses charge conservation at a node. Kirchhoff’s voltage law (KVL) expresses the zero sum of potential differences around a loop in the lumped circuit model. A crossing of wires is connected only when a junction is indicated.

2. Mathematical model

Σ i entering = Σ i leaving; Σ v around a loop = 0

Choose current arrows and a loop traversal direction first. Use the same sign choices throughout.

Explore the illustrated step-by-step circuit solution

3. Worked example

A 12 V source supplies series resistors of 100 Ω and 200 Ω.

  1. KVL gives 12 − 100I − 200I = 0.
  2. Solve I = 12/300 = 0.040 A.
  3. Drops are 4 V and 8 V. Their sum equals the source voltage.
40 mA; 4 V + 8 V = 12 V

4. Write independent equations

In a connected network with n nodes, only n−1 node KCL equations are independent. Adding all node equations cancels every internal branch current. A planar connected circuit with b branches has b−n+1 independent meshes. Selecting independent equations avoids a redundant system and makes the unknown count easy to check.

Analysis method. Label every node and keep the ground symbol as a potential reference, rather than assuming it is a separate current sink.

Second worked example

A connected graph has 4 nodes and 6 branches. Count independent KCL equations and meshes.

3 independent KCL equations; 6−4+1 = 3 meshes in a planar drawing.

Common mistake. KVL signs follow the direction in which you cross each marked voltage polarity.

5. Check your understanding

At a node, 7 mA enters and 2 mA leaves one branch. Find the other outgoing current.

5 mA by KCL.

Must the same current flow through two resistors sharing only one node?

Only if that node has no additional current-carrying branch.

Lecture 05 · Electrical Engineering I

05. Series Networks and Voltage Division

التوالي وتقسيم الجهد

1. Physical understanding

Series elements share a current because their intermediate node has no extra branch. Adding their voltage drops gives the equivalent resistance. Voltage division distributes source voltage in proportion to resistance. A measurement device or load connected across the output creates a parallel path and changes the result. Always include the load before applying the divider formula.

2. Mathematical model

R_eq = R₁ + R₂; V₂ = V_s R₂/(R₁+R₂)

V_s: source voltage (V), V₂: voltage across R₂ (V). The simple divider assumes an unloaded output.

Explore the illustrated step-by-step circuit solution

3. Worked example

A 12 V divider uses R₁ = 1 kΩ and R₂ = 2 kΩ. A 2 kΩ load is connected across R₂.

  1. Unloaded voltage is 12 × 2/3 = 8 V.
  2. Loaded lower resistance is 2 kΩ ∥ 2 kΩ = 1 kΩ.
  3. Loaded output is 12 × 1/(1+1) = 6 V.
8 V unloaded; 6 V loaded

4. Derive the divider

The same current I passes through both resistors. KVL gives V_s = I(R₁+R₂), so I = V_s/(R₁+R₂). Since V₂ = IR₂, substitute I to obtain the divider formula. This derivation explains why the fraction contains the output resistor in its numerator and why a new output branch changes the problem.

Analysis method. Reduce any load in parallel with R₂ first. Apply voltage division using that equivalent resistance.

Second worked example

A 10 V source supplies 2 kΩ and 3 kΩ in series.

I = 2 mA; drops are 4 V and 6 V.

Common mistake. Two elements that appear next to each other in a drawing are not necessarily in series.

5. Check your understanding

Find the output across 3 kΩ in an unloaded 1 kΩ + 3 kΩ divider supplied by 8 V.

6 V.

Why does a finite load lower the output voltage?

It reduces the equivalent lower-leg resistance.

Lecture 06 · Electrical Engineering I

06. Parallel Networks and Current Division

التوازي وتقسيم التيار

1. Physical understanding

Parallel branches connect between the same pair of nodes and therefore share voltage. Total current is the sum of branch currents. Conductances add directly. A small resistance carries more current because it offers greater conductance. The equivalent resistance of positive parallel resistors is smaller than the smallest branch resistance. This inequality is a useful error check.

2. Mathematical model

1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂; I₁ = I_total R₂/(R₁+R₂)

I₁: current through R₁ (A). The two-branch divider uses the opposite branch resistance in its numerator.

Explore the illustrated step-by-step circuit solution

3. Worked example

A 12 V source supplies parallel 600 Ω and 300 Ω resistors.

  1. I₁ = 12/600 = 20 mA.
  2. I₂ = 12/300 = 40 mA.
  3. Total = 60 mA and R_eq = 12/0.060 = 200 Ω.
20 mA; 40 mA; 200 Ω

4. Derive the current divider

Parallel branches share V. KCL gives I_total = V(1/R₁+1/R₂), so V = I_total R₁R₂/(R₁+R₂). Divide that voltage by R₁ to obtain I₁ = I_total R₂/(R₁+R₂). For more branches, use the conductance fraction I_k = I_total G_k/ΣG.

Analysis method. Check the shared pair of nodes, not the orientation of the symbols. Then verify that branch currents sum to the source current.

Second worked example

Three 900 Ω resistors are connected in parallel.

R_eq = 300 Ω. With 9 V, each draws 10 mA and the total is 30 mA.

Common mistake. Current divides inversely with resistance, not directly with resistance.

5. Check your understanding

Divide 9 mA between parallel 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ branches.

6 mA through 1 kΩ and 3 mA through 2 kΩ.

Is a parallel equivalent larger than either branch resistance?

No, for strictly positive finite resistances it is smaller than each branch.

Lecture 07 · Electrical Engineering I

07. Source Models and Circuit Transformations

المصادر وتحويلات الدوائر

1. Physical understanding

An independent source sets a value without referring to another circuit variable. A dependent source is controlled by a voltage or current elsewhere. Keep dependent sources active in analysis. A voltage source in series with a resistance and a current source in parallel with the same resistance can give identical external terminal behaviour. This equivalence does not preserve every internal power. Star–delta conversion preserves the behaviour seen at three external nodes and is useful when direct series–parallel reduction fails.

2. Mathematical model

I_s = V_s/R; R_ab = R_a + R_b + R_aR_b/R_c; R_a = R_abR_ca/(R_ab+R_bc+R_ca)

Star arms R_a, R_b, R_c connect nodes a, b, c to a centre. Delta R_ab joins a to b. Source directions must preserve open-circuit polarity.

3. Worked example

Transform a 10 V source in series with 500 Ω. Convert a star with three 100 Ω arms to delta.

  1. I_s = 10/500 = 20 mA in parallel with 500 Ω.
  2. For the equal star, each delta resistor is 100 + 100 + 100×100/100.
  3. Each delta resistor is 300 Ω.
20 mA ∥ 500 Ω; delta arms 300 Ω

4. Check terminal equivalence

For the voltage-source model, terminal voltage under load current I is V = V_s−IR. For the current-source model, KCL gives V/R = I_s−I. Multiplication by R yields V = I_sR−IR. These terminal equations match when I_sR = V_s. Use the same terminal polarity and outgoing load current in both expressions.

Analysis method. A source transformation requires the associated series or parallel resistance. An ideal voltage source alone has no finite Norton counterpart.

Second worked example

Convert equal delta arms of 900 Ω into star arms.

Each star arm is 900×900/(900+900+900) = 300 Ω.

Common mistake. Do not claim identical internal dissipation merely because the terminal equations match.

5. Check your understanding

Transform a 4 mA source in parallel with 2 kΩ to a voltage-source model.

8 V in series with 2 kΩ, with matching polarity.

Can a dependent source be set to zero in superposition?

No. Only independent sources are deactivated.

Lecture 08 · Electrical Engineering I

08. Nodal Analysis and Supernodes

التحليل العقدي

1. Physical understanding

Choose one reference node and express all other node potentials relative to it. Convert resistor currents to voltage differences divided by resistance, then apply KCL. Current sources enter directly into the node equations. A voltage source between two non-reference nodes creates a supernode: apply KCL to the outer boundary and add the voltage constraint. Nodal analysis works for planar and non-planar circuits.

2. Mathematical model

(V−V_s)/R₁ + V/R₂ = 0; V = V_sR₂/(R₁+R₂)

V: unknown node voltage relative to reference. Each resistor current is written from its start node to its end node.

3. Worked example

A node is connected through 2 kΩ to a 10 V node and through 3 kΩ to ground.

  1. Write (V−10)/2000 + V/3000 = 0.
  2. Multiply by 6000: 3(V−10) + 2V = 0.
  3. Solve 5V = 30, so V = 6 V. Branch currents are both 2 mA.
6 V; 2 mA

4. Solve a two-node system

Let node A connect to 12 V through 1 kΩ, to ground through 2 kΩ and to node B through 1 kΩ. Let B also connect to ground through 1 kΩ. Expressing currents in mA gives 2.5V_A−V_B = 12 and −V_A+2V_B = 0. Hence V_A = 6 V and V_B = 3 V. The supply current is 6 mA, split into 3 mA to ground and 3 mA toward B.

Analysis method. Write one KCL equation per unknown node. Retain terms for resistors joining two unknown nodes.

Second worked example

A 2 mA source injects into a node with two 2 kΩ resistors to ground.

Equivalent resistance is 1 kΩ, so the node is at 2 V. Each resistor carries 1 mA.

Common mistake. A reference node is arbitrary. Changing it changes reported node voltages but not branch voltage differences.

5. Check your understanding

A node connects to 12 V through 3 kΩ and ground through 1 kΩ. Find its voltage.

3 V. Substitute in KCL to verify both currents are 3 mA.

What extra equation is required for a supernode?

The voltage-source constraint linking its two node voltages.

Lecture 09 · Electrical Engineering I

09. Mesh Analysis and Supermeshes

التحليل الحلقي

1. Physical understanding

A mesh is a loop containing no smaller loop in a planar drawing. Assign one mesh current per mesh and write KVL. The current in a shared resistor is the difference of adjacent mesh currents if both circulate clockwise. A current source shared between meshes produces a supermesh. Write KVL around its outer perimeter and add the source-current constraint. Mesh analysis applies directly to planar circuits, whereas general loop analysis is broader.

2. Mathematical model

(R₁+R₃)I₁ − R₃I₂ = V_s; −R₃I₁ + (R₂+R₃)I₂ = 0

R₃: shared resistance. I₁ and I₂: clockwise mesh currents (A).

3. Worked example

Two meshes have R₁ = R₂ = R₃ = 1 kΩ. Only mesh 1 contains a 6 V source.

  1. Equations are 2000I₁−1000I₂ = 6 and −1000I₁+2000I₂ = 0.
  2. The second equation gives I₁ = 2I₂.
  3. Substitution gives I₂ = 2 mA, I₁ = 4 mA and shared current 2 mA.
I₁ = 4 mA; I₂ = 2 mA

4. Use a supermesh constraint

If a 3 mA current source is shared between two clockwise meshes and its arrow follows mesh 1 on the common branch, the constraint is I₁−I₂ = 3 mA. Write one KVL equation around the outer boundary, omitting the unknown source voltage. Solve that equation together with the current constraint. The source arrow determines the sign.

Analysis method. Mark the current-source arrow before writing its constraint. Check branch currents after solving the mesh currents.

Second worked example

Mesh currents are 5 mA and 2 mA clockwise. A shared resistor is 500 Ω.

Shared current is 3 mA in the direction of mesh 1; its corresponding drop is 1.5 V.

Common mistake. A mesh current is an analysis variable. It need not equal any measured current in a shared branch.

5. Check your understanding

In the same circuit replace the source by 9 V. Find both mesh currents.

I₁ = 6 mA and I₂ = 3 mA.

Why is the shared resistor voltage not simply R₃I₁?

Both mesh currents pass through it, so its branch current is I₁−I₂.

Lecture 10 · Electrical Engineering I

10. Superposition, Thévenin and Norton

التراكب وثيفنن ونورتن

1. Physical understanding

Superposition adds signed responses due to independent sources one at a time in a linear circuit. Deactivate an ideal voltage source by shorting it and an ideal current source by opening it. Power is nonlinear in current, so calculate total current or voltage before finding power. Thévenin replaces a linear one-port with its open-circuit voltage in series with resistance. Norton uses short-circuit current in parallel with the same resistance. With dependent sources, determine resistance using a test source after deactivating independent sources.

2. Mathematical model

V_th = V_oc; R_th = V_test/I_test; I_N = V_th/R_th; I_L = V_th/(R_th+R_L)

V_oc: open-circuit voltage, R_L: load resistance. Positive finite R_th is assumed for the load formulas here.

3. Worked example

A 12 V divider has 1 kΩ from source to output and 2 kΩ from output to ground. Find its Thévenin model and the current in a 1 kΩ load.

  1. Open-circuit voltage = 12×2/3 = 8 V.
  2. Short the independent source: R_th = 1 kΩ ∥ 2 kΩ = 666.67 Ω.
  3. I_L = 8/(666.67+1000) = 4.8 mA.
V_th = 8 V; R_th = 666.67 Ω; I_L = 4.8 mA

4. Keep a test source when needed

After removing the load, compute open-circuit terminal voltage with all sources active. To find resistance, deactivate independent sources. When dependent sources remain, attach a test voltage and calculate the resulting current, or use a test current and calculate voltage. The ratio gives input resistance. Do not use a passive resistor reduction if the active dependent source changes the port relation.

Analysis method. Open the load before finding V_th. Reattach it only after the port equivalent is established.

Second worked example

A linear port has V_oc = 15 V and I_sc = 5 mA.

R_th = 15/0.005 = 3 kΩ and the Norton source is 5 mA.

Common mistake. A short circuit for finding I_sc is an analysis operation. Never short a real supply without appropriate laboratory procedures.

5. Check your understanding

A Thévenin source is 6 V in series with 2 kΩ. Find its Norton equivalent.

3 mA in parallel with 2 kΩ.

Can individual source powers be added to obtain total resistor power?

No. First add the signed voltages or currents, then compute power.

Lecture 11 · Electrical Engineering I

11. Maximum Power and Further Network Theorems

أقصى قدرة ونظريات الشبكات

1. Physical understanding

For a positive resistive Thévenin source, load power first rises with load resistance and then falls. Its maximum occurs at resistance matching. At the maximum, the internal source resistor dissipates the same power as the load, giving 50% efficiency in this model. Millman combines parallel source-resistor branches using conductance weighting. Substitution preserves the operating state by replacing a branch with a source having its existing voltage or current. Reciprocity interchanges excitation and response in a linear bilateral network under the applicable source and measurement conditions.

2. Mathematical model

P_L = V_th²R_L/(R_th+R_L)²; dP_L/dR_L = V_th²(R_th−R_L)/(R_th+R_L)³; V = Σ(V_k/R_k)/Σ(1/R_k)

V_k: signed source voltage in parallel branch k, R_k: its series resistance. Maximum-power formula assumes R_th > 0.

3. Worked example

A Thévenin source is 10 V with 100 Ω resistance. Find the optimum load and maximum power.

  1. Set the derivative of load power to zero.
  2. R_L = R_th = 100 Ω.
  3. P_max = 10²/(4×100) = 0.25 W; load voltage is 5 V.
100 Ω; 0.25 W

4. Interpret matching and efficiency

At R_L = R_th, current is V_th/(2R_th), so the load and internal resistor each dissipate V_th²/(4R_th). Efficiency is P_L/(P_L+P_internal) = R_L/(R_th+R_L). Raising R_L increases this efficiency but eventually lowers delivered load power. Thus the best choice depends on whether the specification prioritises power or energy efficiency.

Analysis method. Check R_th > 0 before using the simple matching result. An active negative-resistance model requires separate treatment.

Second worked example

A 12 V source has 300 Ω internal resistance. Find P_max.

R_L = 300 Ω and P_max = 144/1200 = 0.12 W.

Common mistake. Reciprocity is not a general property of all circuits. Its conditions exclude unilateral active behaviour.

5. Check your understanding

Use Millman for 12 V through 1 kΩ and 6 V through 2 kΩ into the same unloaded node.

10 V: (12/1000+6/2000)/(1/1000+1/2000).

Does maximum load power imply maximum efficiency?

No. Matching gives 50% efficiency in the positive resistive Thévenin model.

Lecture 12 · Electrical Engineering I

12. Magnetic Circuits and DC Measurements

الدوائر المغناطيسية والقياسات

1. Physical understanding

A coil produces magnetomotive force NI. In a uniform linear magnetic path, field strength is NI divided by path length and flux density is permeability times field strength. Reluctance plays a role analogous to resistance, while magnetic flux is analogous to current. Real cores have nonlinear magnetization, saturation and leakage. Use the magnetization curve for nonlinear cores. In a series magnetic path flux is common, while magnetomotive-force drops add. A small air gap can dominate reluctance. Measure voltage in parallel, current in series, and resistance only after power is removed.

2. Mathematical model

ℱ = NI; H = NI/ℓ; B = μH; Φ = BA; ℛ = ℓ/(μA); Φ = ℱ/Σℛ

N: turns, I: current (A), H: field strength (A/m), μ: permeability (H/m), Φ: flux (Wb), ℛ: reluctance (A turns/Wb).

3. Worked example

A linear core has N = 200, I = 0.1 A, ℓ = 0.2 m, A = 1 cm² and μ_r = 1000.

  1. Convert area to 10⁻⁴ m² and use μ = μ₀μ_r with μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m.
  2. H = 200×0.1/0.2 = 100 A/m.
  3. B = 0.12566 T and Φ = 0.12566×10⁻⁴ = 12.566 μWb.
H = 100 A/m; B = 0.12566 T; Φ = 12.566 μWb

4. Account for gaps and practical meters

For a core of length ℓ_c and an air gap ℓ_g with common cross-section A, neglecting fringing gives total reluctance ℓ_c/(μ_cA)+ℓ_g/(μ₀A). Use Φ = NI/ℛ_total. For parallel magnetic paths, each path has the same magnetomotive-force difference, while fluxes add. Leakage flux means useful flux is smaller than total produced flux. In electrical measurements, a voltmeter has large but finite input resistance and an ammeter has small but finite series resistance.

Analysis method. Inspect the B–H curve before assuming constant permeability. State explicitly when leakage and air-gap fringing are neglected.

Second worked example

A 1000-turn coil carries 20 mA. Find its magnetomotive force.

ℱ = 1000 × 0.020 = 20 A turns.

Common mistake. Never connect an ammeter directly across a voltage source. Its low resistance can cause a large current.

5. Check your understanding

How should a voltmeter and ammeter be connected to measure a resistor?

Voltmeter in parallel and ammeter in series. Start with a suitable measurement range.

Why can an air gap dominate reluctance?

Air permeability is much lower than the permeability of an unsaturated magnetic core.
