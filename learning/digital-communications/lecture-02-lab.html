2.1 What changes in a pulse?

Pulse modulation represents a message by changing a property of a pulse train. Pulse-amplitude modulation (PAM) changes height. Pulse-width modulation (PWM), also called pulse-duration modulation (PDM), changes width. Pulse-position modulation (PPM) changes timing.

PWM and PPM belong to pulse-time modulation (PTM). These are analog pulse methods because the varied parameter can take a continuous range of values. A train of pulses is not automatically a digital code.

MethodVaried propertyFixed properties in this model
PAMHeightWidth and nominal sampling times
PWMWidthHeight and leading edge
PPMPositionHeight and width

2.2 Sampling: choose the measurement times

Let x(t) be the continuous-time message. At t = kTs, measure its value x(kTs). The integer k counts the samples. Ts is the sampling interval and fs is the sampling rate.

T_s=\frac1{f_s},\qquad x[k]=x(kT_s),\qquad f_s>2B

B is the highest baseband message frequency. The Nyquist boundary is 2B. Use a higher rate in practice to allow a transition band for the filters. Sampling exactly at twice a sinusoid frequency can lose information for particular phases.

Example 2.1 · A 3 kHz message sampled at 8 kHz

f_N=2B=2(3)=6\;\mathrm{kHz}

The chosen rate is above the Nyquist boundary because 8 > 6.

T_s=1/8000=125\;\mu\mathrm{s}

A sample is taken every 125 μs.

2.3 Natural and flat-top PAM

Natural PAM multiplies the message by a periodic gate. During each open gate, the top of the pulse follows the changing message.

x_{nat}(t)=x(t)p(t)

Flat-top PAM takes one value at the sampling instant and holds that value for a finite pulse duration τ. The top is horizontal even when the message continues to change.

x_{flat}(t)=\sum_k x(kT_s)h(t-kT_s)

h(t) is a unit-height rectangular pulse lasting τ seconds. The duty ratio is d = τ/Ts. Reducing τ narrows a pulse without changing the sampling interval.

2.4 Spectral replicas and recovery

Sampling creates shifted copies of the message spectrum around integer multiples of fs. If copies overlap, a low-pass filter cannot separate them without ambiguity.

X_s(f)=f_s\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}X(f-kf_s)

For natural PAM using a unit-height gate with duty d, the zero-frequency Fourier-series coefficient of the gate is d. With separated replicas, an ideal LPF extracts d x(t). A gain of 1/d restores the amplitude.

2.5 Flat-top holding and aperture effect

A finite hold pulse has a frequency-dependent response. Its magnitude falls with frequency, so higher-frequency message components can be attenuated more strongly.

H(f)=\tau\operatorname{sinc}(f\tau)e^{-j\pi f\tau},\qquad \operatorname{sinc}(u)=\frac{\sin(\pi u)}{\pi u}

The normalized aperture magnitude is |sinc(fτ)|. The exponential term represents delay. A shorter hold duration reduces amplitude droop within a fixed message band. An equalizer can compensate in the useful band, but cannot restore information at a response zero.

Aperture magnitude at 3 kHz with τ = 20 μs

f\tau=3000(20\times10^{-6})=0.06

|\operatorname{sinc}(0.06)|\simeq0.99409

The normalized amplitude is approximately 99.41% of its low-frequency value.

2.6 PWM: encode a value as a duration

In a leading-edge-fixed PWM model, a pulse starts at kTs and ends after a duration τk. Larger message samples produce wider pulses when the sensitivity is positive.

\tau_k=\tau_0+k_w x(kT_s)

τ0 is the width for a zero sample. kw is the time-per-volt sensitivity. Valid widths must remain positive and smaller than the available slot.

A comparator can compare a held sample with a rising ramp. The crossing sets the falling edge. A fixed leading edge and a message-dependent falling edge create the width variation.

2.7 PPM: encode a value as a delay

In this model, the PPM pulse begins at the falling edge of the PWM pulse. A one-shot circuit generates a pulse of fixed duration. Thus height and width stay constant while the pulse position changes.

d_k=\tau_0+k_wx(kT_s),\qquad t_{PPM,k}=kT_s+d_k

dk is the delay inside slot k. It is not the absolute starting time. For fixed PPM width τp, require 0 ≤ dk and dk + τp ≤ Ts.

A PPM pulse in slot k = 2

Use T_s = 100 μs, d_k = 74 μs and pulse width 10 μs.

t_{start}=2(100)+74=274\;\mu\mathrm{s}

t_{end}=274+10=284\;\mu\mathrm{s}

The slot ends at 300 μs, so the pulse fits.

2.8 Recover the sample from width or position

Measure the PWM duration, or measure the PPM delay relative to the synchronized slot reference. Then invert the modulation equation.

x(kT_s)=\frac{d_k-\tau_0}{k_w}

A PPM receiver needs a timing reference. A change in the reference time can look like a change in the message. Timing jitter therefore affects the recovered sample even when pulse height is unchanged.

Recover a sample from a delay of 65 μs

Use τ₀ = 50 μs and k_w = 30 μs/V.

x=(65-50)/30=0.5\;\mathrm{V}

A 1 μs timing error would cause a sample error of 1/30 V, about 0.0333 V.

Why does PPM require synchronization?

2.9 Compare the practical trade-offs

PAM carries information in amplitude, so amplitude disturbances directly affect its information-bearing property. PWM and PPM can use amplitude limiting because their information is carried by timing. Timing errors still matter.

Fixed-height, fixed-width PPM pulses have constant pulse energy in this ideal model. PWM pulse energy varies with width. Neither statement alone determines the complete system power efficiency or noise performance.

Narrow rectangular pulses contain significant high-frequency components. Their ideal spectra extend indefinitely. A quoted bandwidth must specify a criterion such as the first spectral null or an occupied-power bandwidth. Do not treat pulse repetition frequency as the complete transmission bandwidth.

2.10 Frequency, bandwidth and sampling problems

Convert angular frequency ω in rad/s to f = ω/(2π) in hertz. In a sum, identify the largest frequency that remains. Multiplication in time convolves spectra, so the support limits can add.

A sum of sinusoids

x(t)=3\cos(200\pi t)+2\sin(600\pi t)

f_1=100\;\mathrm{Hz},\qquad f_2=300\;\mathrm{Hz}

f_N=2(300)=600\;\mathrm{sample/s}

Normalized sinc signals

\operatorname{sinc}(100t): B=50\;\mathrm{Hz},\quad f_N=100\;\mathrm{sample/s}

\operatorname{sinc}^2(100t): B=50+50=100\;\mathrm{Hz},\quad f_N=200\;\mathrm{sample/s}

\operatorname{sinc}(100t)+3\operatorname{sinc}^2(60t): B=\max(50,60)=60\;\mathrm{Hz}

f_N=120\;\mathrm{sample/s},\quad T_N=8.333\;\mathrm{ms}

Two filtered signals are multiplied

The first filter has cutoff 20000π rad/s. The second has cutoff 10000π rad/s. Assume both output spectra fill their passbands.

B_1=10000\;\mathrm{Hz},\quad B_2=5000\;\mathrm{Hz}

B_{product}=15000\;\mathrm{Hz},\quad f_{N,product}=30000\;\mathrm{sample/s}

Adding the outputs instead would have a bandwidth of 10 kHz.

2.11 Aliasing: two signals, identical samples

Sampling does not distinguish sinusoidal frequencies that differ by an integer multiple of the sampling rate. Choose an alias in the baseband interval between −f_s/2 and +f_s/2.

f_a=|f-kf_s|,\qquad 0\leq f_a\leq f_s/2

Example 2.5 · 100 Hz cosine sampled at 150 Hz

f_a=|100-150|=50\;\mathrm{Hz}

The original and the alias agree at every sample instant. A reconstruction filter cannot infer which one was originally present without further information.

2.12 Natural PAM: weights of spectral copies

For unit-height rectangular gates centred on each sampling instant, the Fourier-series coefficients are:

c_n=d\operatorname{sinc}(nd),\quad c_0=d,\qquad X_{PAM}(f)=\sum_n c_nX(f-nf_s)

Shifting the gates changes coefficient phases. The centred-gate formula above has no additional time-shift phase. A missing spectral copy does not mean all higher copies disappear.

Example 2.7 · Gate width T_s/3

d=1/3,\quad c_0=1/3,\quad c_n=\tfrac13\operatorname{sinc}(n/3)

The first zeros occur at n = ±3. Restore the central-copy amplitude with a gain of three.

Example 2.8 · Gate width T_s/2

c_n=\tfrac12\operatorname{sinc}(n/2)

Nonzero even-indexed copies vanish. The central copy has gain 1/2, so recovery gain is two.

2.13 Convolution and sinc interpolation

A weighted impulse shifted in time produces a shifted and scaled hold pulse after convolution:

a\delta(t-t_0)*h(t)=ah(t-t_0)

A height-two held sample

Let h(t) last 10 μs. Convolving 2δ(t − 30 μs) with h(t) gives 2h(t − 30 μs).

The resulting pulse has height two and extends from 30 to 40 μs.

Under the bandlimiting and ideal sampling conditions, an infinite sinc interpolation sum reconstructs the signal:

x(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x(nT_s)\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{t-nT_s}{T_s}\right)

At a sampling instant, the matching sinc equals one and every other sinc equals zero. The finite sum in the animation illustrates the construction, with truncation error near the edges.

2.14 Question bank · 25 solved questions

Question 1 · What changes in PAM, PWM and PPM?

PAM changes pulse height.

PWM changes pulse width.

PPM changes position. Its height and width remain fixed.

Question 2 · Compare natural and flat-top PAM.

Natural PAM follows the message during an open gate.

Flat-top PAM holds the value measured at the sampling instant.

Question 3 · Explain sample, hold and reset.

Sample measures the input.

Hold preserves the measured value.

Reset returns a return-to-zero PAM output to zero. A zero-order hold instead keeps the sample until the next sample.

Question 4 · Find the Nyquist rate for a highest frequency of 2 kHz.

f_N=2B=2(2)=4\;\mathrm{ksample/s}

Use a higher practical sampling rate.

Question 5 · Find the period for a sampling rate of 10 kHz.

T_s=1/f_s=1/10000=100\;\mu\mathrm{s}

Question 6 · Find the Nyquist rate for 3cos(200πt) + 2sin(600πt).

The two frequencies are 100 Hz and 300 Hz.

f_N=2(300)=600\;\mathrm{sample/s}

Question 7 · Why filter before sampling?

Remove unwanted high-frequency components before they fold into the wanted band.

A later LPF cannot generally undo aliasing.

Question 8 · Find the baseband alias of a 700 Hz cosine sampled at 1000 Hz.

f_a=|700-1000|=300\;\mathrm{Hz}

Question 9 · Choose an ideal reconstruction cutoff for B = 2 kHz and fs = 8 kHz.

The next copy starts at fs − B = 6 kHz.

Choose 2 < fc < 6 kHz, for example 4 kHz.

Question 10 · Find duty and recovery gain for τ = 25 μs and Ts = 100 μs.

d=25/100=0.25

G=1/d=4

Question 11 · For centred gates with d = 1/2, find c₀ and the first missing copy.

c_n=d\operatorname{sinc}(nd),\quad c_0=1/2

The first nonzero n with nd = 1 is n = 2. Copies at ±2fs vanish.

Question 12 · List the natural-PAM blocks and functions.

Anti-alias filter limits input bandwidth.

Sampling switch gates the message.

Receiver LPF selects the central spectrum.

Gain 1/d restores amplitude.

Question 13 · Find 2δ(t − 30 μs) * h(t) for a 10 μs unit hold pulse.

g(t)=2h(t-30\;\mu\mathrm{s})

A height-two rectangle begins at 30 μs and ends at 40 μs.

Question 14 · What is aperture effect? Find the first null for τ = 20 μs.

A finite hold introduces frequency-dependent attenuation.

f_{null}=1/\tau=1/(20\times10^{-6})=50\;\mathrm{kHz}

Question 15 · Why does sinc interpolation reproduce a sample exactly?

At the matching sample instant, the sinc argument is zero and its value is one.

All other sinc arguments are nonzero integers and their values are zero.

Question 16 · Use τ = 50 + 20x μs. Find widths for x = −1, 0, +1 V.

For −1 V: 50 − 20 = 30 μs.

For 0 V: 50 μs.

For +1 V: 50 + 20 = 70 μs.

Question 17 · A ramp rises 0–4 V in 100 μs. Find width for a 2 V control.

\tau=T_s(V_c/V_r)=100(2/4)=50\;\mu\mathrm{s}

Question 18 · A centred PWM pulse has centre 60 μs and width 40 μs. Find its edges.

Half-width = 20 μs.

Start = 60 − 20 = 40 μs.

End = 60 + 20 = 80 μs.

Question 19 · Generate PPM from fixed-leading-edge PWM.

Detect the moving falling edge.

Trigger a fixed-width, fixed-height pulse at that edge.

Question 20 · Find x for delay 70 μs, baseline 50 μs and sensitivity 20 μs/V.

x=(d-\tau_0)/k_w=(70-50)/20=1\;\mathrm{V}

Question 21 · How does a set-reset flip-flop convert PPM to PWM?

The slot reference sets its output high.

The PPM leading edge resets its output low.

The high interval equals the PPM delay.

Question 22 · Find timing error for a +0.02 V disturbance and rising slope 0.1 V/μs.

\Delta t\simeq-v_n/q=-0.02/0.1=-0.2\;\mu\mathrm{s}

The crossing occurs about 0.2 μs earlier. This is a local linear approximation.

Question 23 · Find Nyquist rates for sinc(100t) and sinc²(100t).

Normalized sinc(100t) has B = 50 Hz, so fN = 100 samples/s.

Squaring adds spectral supports: B = 100 Hz, so fN = 200 samples/s.

Question 24 · Two signals fill 2 kHz and 3 kHz bands. Find the product Nyquist rate.

B_{product}=2+3=5\;\mathrm{kHz}

f_N=2(5)=10\;\mathrm{ksample/s}

Question 25 · Draw pulses for samples −1, 0, +1 V at 0, 100, 200 μs. Use PAM width 20 μs, PWM width 50 + 20x μs, PPM width 10 μs.

PAM heights are −1, 0, +1 V. A zero sample is valid even though no pulse is visible.

PWM widths are 30, 50, 70 μs.

PPM starts are 30, 150, 270 μs.

PPM ends are 40, 160, 280 μs.
